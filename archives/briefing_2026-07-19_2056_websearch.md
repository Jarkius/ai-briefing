## 🎯 Prompt Engineering Tips

### 2026 Prompt Engineering Principles

#### 1. Structured Four-Layer Prompts Outperform Single Strings
Modern best practice uses **four explicit layers**:
- **System layer**: Role and behavioral guidelines
- **Developer layer**: Technical constraints and output format
- **Context layer**: Background information and examples
- **User layer**: Specific request

This structure on Claude Opus 4.7 **lifts SWE-bench scores 23 points** vs single-string prompts.

#### 2. Prompt Caching Cuts Costs 70–90%
Cache your system prompts, long context, and examples. On Claude and GPT-5.6, proper caching reduces costs by **70–90%** for repeated workflows.

#### 3. Modern Models Are Context-Sensitive, Not Intent-Deficient
Claude 4.6, GPT-5, and Gemini 2.5 understand intent better but are **more sensitive to context overload**. The problem shifted from "how do I make it understand?" to "how do I give it the right information?"

#### 4. Tell the Model What You Want to See
If you want comprehensive output, **ask for it explicitly**. If you want specific features, **list them**. Modern models benefit from directness over cleverness.

#### 5. Scaffolding Over Vague Wording
Define: role, task, context, constraints, examples, and output format. Write prompts as **reusable decision rules**, not one-off requests.

#### 6. Avoid Hallucinations Through Constraints
- Limit the scope of the response
- Provide factual grounding material
- Ask the model to cite sources
- Use structured output formats (JSON, tables)

### Anti-Patterns to Avoid
❌ Vague instructions: "Make it better"  
❌ Assuming context without providing it  
❌ Asking for precision without examples  
❌ Overloading with irrelevant details  

✅ Specific requests: "Rewrite this email to be 50% shorter while keeping the project timeline and next steps"  
✅ Explicit context: "You are a technical writer for a SaaS company. Our audience is IT decision-makers."  
✅ Examples first: "Here are 3 good examples... now write one for this case"
